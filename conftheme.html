Minority Pressure

Fahrenheit 451 is not really about censorship as much as it is about self-censorship. The book reveals that the government didn't initially ban books by force, as is often the case in dystopias. Instead, various social groups condemned certain books because they were offensive or didn’t conform to their worldviews. Writers — and later, the government — in this book wanted to prevent offending any "minority" imaginable. Much of my evidence for this is from Beatty's speech from pages 54 to 59, which explicitly confirms the points I make, but I will also reference text from other areas.

On page 54, Beatty said to Montag during his monologue, Don't step on the toes of the dog-lovers, the cat-lovers, doctors, lawyers, merchants, chiefs, Mormons, Baptists, Unitarians, second-generation Chinese, Swedes, Italians, Germans, Texans, Brooklynites, Irishmen, people from Oregon or Mexico. This quote explains why the astounding majority of books have been supressed instead of a select few. Many have heard of the saying, "You will always offend someone, no matter what you do." The same concept applies to books. All books will undeniably contain content that will upset at least one group of people. For example, a seemingly non-problematic book like Harry Potter gets backlash from religious people for depicting witchcraft. However, because writers are set on the goal of keeping all its audience content, it would better fit their goal to hinder the release all potentially uncomfortable content than allow content that will upset some.

Why was the government so eager about not offending minorities? Could they have really posed that much of a threat? On page 56, Beatty said, You must understand that our civilization is so vast that we can't have our minorities upset and stirred. This implies that, among society, the minority populations are large enough (either individually or collectively) that if conflict is stirred, it might have more impactful fallout.


Superficiality and the Destruction of Discomfort

As "upsetting" books, with ideas regarded as "evil", "controversial," "confusing," and the like, were shunned and destroyed, books and other media lacking such boomed in popularity. People stopped writing about topics that could upset their audience because they didn't sell, and instead focused on shallow, saccharine stories. Authors, full of evil thoughts, lock up your typewriters. They did. Magazines became a nice blend of vanilla tapioca, claimed Beatty on page 55.

In fact, it was not just books that have been censored. Books are only one type of media that holds ideas that may be uncomfortable or upsetting to certain people. For example, funerals were eliminated because they were "unhappy" (as well as "pagan"). Instead, bodies are burnt into ashes and are forgotten, because mourning brings depression. Even politics are ignored because they can spark anger or apprehension. People would much rather focus on comfort and immediate gratification, but push aside emotional depth, critical thought, and considering issues that may be uncomfortable, sure, but still affect them, their community, and generations to come.

On page 97, Mrs. Phelps' breakdown and Mrs. Bowle's commentary emphasizes that generally, society reacts to content emotionally, not logically. So, if the media is happy, people accept it, regardless of its depth or importance. If the media is upsetting, people will reject and seek to get rid of it so that others cannot access it, also regardless of its significance. If it evokes bad feelings, then it must be bad. When Mrs. Phelps wept at Dover Beach: The Sea of Faith after Montag read it aloud, it was used as proof that books are dangerous. Was she weeping at the poem's content? Was she distraught not because of the content, but because of the book itself, indoctrinated to hate them? Regardless, during this event, Mrs. Bowles said, I've always said, poetry and tears, poetry and suicide and crying and awful feelings, poetry and sickness; all that mush!

Intellectualism also strikes a feeling of jealousy or inequality. Beatty explained on page 55 that the majority feels insecure about their intelligence. Comparing themselves to others who are "better" hurts — you and I know. That alone implies that people do believe intelligence is a good trait. They believe it makes someone exceptional. And it pains people to know that there are others "smarter" than them. Instead of striving to become as bright as the people they compare themselves to, or not allowing other people's achievements to discredit themselves, people instead takes out their insecurity on intellectuals, whom they view as pretentious snobs, so that they don't have anyone to compare themselves to. So, intellectuals are targeted and shunned as well, and to encourage uniformity, thought-provoking books are destroyed.



Was It Really Worth It?

No.

What Ray Bradbury seems to be saying is:

Just because something is offensive, uncomfortable, or doesn't conform to your worldview, doesn't mean it should be eradicated, as it could bring more harm than good. Additionally, anyone can be a threat to book access, not just the government.

Uncomfortable books hold plenty of value in educating readers about alternative worldviews and helping readers learn and recognize patterns in history, practice critical thinking, and maybe even develop tolerance. Some books may be genuinely offensive to minorities, using stereotypes as a gimmick or encouraging harmful ideology. People have all the right to have strong feelings about such content, especially if it has a negative real-world impact (has a book ever made anyone racist?). However, in Fahrenheit 451, there is no mention of a book being banned merely because it has actually influenced people to adopt bigoted worldviews and perpetuate harm. And they have outlawed not just offensive books, but literally every book in the country except entertaining comic books and magazines. This, paired with the new education system, leaves society very uneducated beyond trivia such as how much corn Iowa grew last year, and thus susceptible to manipulation. This also leaves them very unaware about politics and the history that impacts it, so they are left vulnerable to government exploitation.
