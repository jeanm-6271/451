Nazi Germany Book Burnings

In the early 1930s, millions of Germans adopted Nazi beliefs, supported the Nazi party, and accepted Hitler as chancellor in January 1933. In May 1933, ceremonious book burnings were performed across the country. These book burnings, however, were not organized by the Nazi government. Pro-Nazi university students, with the goal of removing "un-German" (undeutsch) books and promoting Nazism, hosted them themselves. In both the Nazi regime and Fahrenheit 451, society shunned certain books and supported their destruction, albeit for different reasons. The German university students parallel the firemen, as they instigate the book burnings. The onlookers represent neighbours gathering around the burning, intrigued by the event.

Just as they were in Fahrenheit 451, Nazi book burnings were like a circus show — as in, they were like spectacles. Nazi book burnings usually had rallies, speeches, and torchlit city parades. For example, during a book burning in Konigsplatz, Munich, 3,000 people joined university students marching through the streets with torch. The gathering recited Nazi songs and Gegenbach, the event's main leader, addressed the crowd with a speceh. Book burnings were even broadcast live on radio. Although book burnings in Fahrenheit 451 were not as celebrated and prepared for as the ones in Nazi Germany, they both still entertained their bystanders, even though it was an event of destruction.

The DSt, which was a national umbrella organization of German university student committees, instructed students to "cleanse" "un-German" books from their collections, their friends' collections, and even collections at public libraries. The reporting system in Fahrenheit 451 is similar. In Fahrenheit 451, people can and are urged to report their neighbors to the firemen if they suspect that they are keeping books in their homes. In Nazi Germany, ordinary people could do the same thing. They could report neighbors, coworkers, friends, and family to the Gestapo for owning Jewish-authored literature, as it was viewed as treason or disloyalty, just as owning books were in Fahrenheit 451. Nazi organizations, just like firemen, raided homes to take and inflame books (as well as bookstores and libraries).

Of course, there are several differences between Nazi and Fahrenheit 451 book burnings. Nazi Germany specifically targeted books with Jewish authors, books that promoted leftist movements (such as communism and socialism), war-criticizing pacifist books, and books that condemned Nazism. The firemen targeted all sorts of books. Nazi Germany aimed to eliminate Jewish works and influence, and re-affirm and promote Nazi ideology. The firemen aimed to prevent upset among society. However, book burnings during Nazi Germany are similar to book burnings in Fahrenheit 451 in the sense that the censoring and burning of books were voluntarily supported by the people, and the government only aided the people in it later on.
